We instantiate the state-based OR-Set detailed in figure 2 of [Bieniusa et al.](cite:cites optimized-set-crdt) over RDF 1.2,
setting elements as RDF 1.2 triples and unique tags as [UUDv4](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9562.html) identifiers.
RDF 1.2 triple terms allow a triple to appear in the object position of another triple,
enabling statements to be made about triples directly.
We use this to link each tracked triple, its add-tags, and its tombstones through an identifying (blank) node.
A triple is considered present when it has at least one add-tag not in its tombstone set.
On merge, multiple identifying nodes for the same triple are consolidated into one.
[](#transformer) shows an example CRDT dataset.

### NTP Tombstone Optimization

As noted in our related work, Bieniusa et al.'s tombstone optimization requires causal delivery and a known replica set —
assumptions that do not hold in open decentralized ecosystems.
We instead exploit NTP synchronization and assume all replicas live on NTP synclonized machines,
which is a better fit to the Web based open decentralized ecosystems:
since [NTP](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5905) enters panic mode after a drift exceeding $$1000\text{s}$$,
any two synchronized clients differ by at most $$2000\text{s} \approx 33.5\text{min}$$.
By attaching the current timestamp whenever a tag is created, or when it is tombstoned,
by using `crdt:stamp-uuid` — formatted as `{uuid}--{xsd:dateTime}` —
a CRDT dataset can declare a synchronization interval $$d$$, after which stale metadata is pruned:
1. an add-tag may be dropped if a newer add-tag exists and all replicas have seen it;
2. a tombstone may be dropped if all replicas have seen it; and
3. the identifying node may be dropped if no other triple references it.
A tag is considered seen by all replicas once its timestamp is older than $$d + 4000\text{s}$$.
This bound is derived as follows: consider machine A at drift $$+1000\text{s}$$ that stalls for $$2000\text{s}$$ — still within NTP tolerance —
and machine B at drift $$-1000\text{s}$$ that subsequently jumps $$+2000\text{s}$$.
In the worst case, B adds a tag at $$t_0$$, which is instantly seen by A after which it freezes.
A will next sync at $$t_0 + d + 2000\text{s}$$, but due to B's maximum forward jump,
B's clock reads $$t_0 + d + 4000\text{s}$$ at that moment — hence the bound.
