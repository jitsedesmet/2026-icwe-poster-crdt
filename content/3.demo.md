## State-based Add-Wins CRDT-RDF
{:#core}

<!-- https://katex.org/docs/supported.html -->


We instantiate the state-based OR-Set of [Bieniusa et al.](cite:cites optimized-set-crdt) (Figure 2) over RDF 1.2,
setting elements as RDF 1.2 triples and unique tags as [UUDv4](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9562.html) identifiers.
Using RDF 1.2 triple terms, a triple can be stored in the object position of another triple, as such, statements can be made about triples.
Using this functionity, we link triples, tags and thumbstones through a triple identifing (blank) node.
CRDT-aware clients assert a triple when it has at least one add-tag.
When an element in the set has multiple identifying nodes, they get merged into one.

### Time optimization


As noted in our related work, Bieniusa et al.'s tombstone optimization requires causal delivery and a known replica set —
assumptions that do not hold in open decentralized ecosystems.
We instead exploit NTP synchronization and assume all replicas live on NTP synclonized machines, which is a better fit to the Web based open decentralized ecosystems:
since NTP enters panic mode after a drift exceeding 1000s,
any two syncronized clients differ by at most $$2000\text{s} \approx 33.5\text{min}$$.
A CRDT dataset can therefore declare a synchronization interval $$d\text{s}$$, declaring all clients must syncronize $$d$$ seconds.
By using the datatype `crdt:stamp-uuid`, whose values are formatted as `{uuid}--{xsd datetime}`, and stammping whenever a new tag is created, or when a tag is added to the tombstones,
we then optimize as follows:
1. an add-tag may be dropped once a newer add-tag exists and all replicas have seen it;
2. a tombstone may be dropped once all replicas have seen it; and
3. the identifying blank node may be dropped if no other triple references it.
A tag is considered universally seen once its timestamp is older than $$d + 4000\text{s}$$ —
accounting for the worst-case NTP drift between any two replicas:
Consider a machine (A) starting at a drift of $$ +1000\text{s} $$ may stall for $$ 2000\text{s} $$ and still function correctly according to NTP, meanwhile a system (B)
may start at a drift of $$ -1000\text{s} $$ and may jump $$ +2000\text{s} $$ and still be correct.
As such the worse case is when A and B sync, then B adds a tag and A freezes for $$ 2000 $$ seconds, since it only needs to sync every $$ d $$ seconds, it will sync at timestamp $$ t_0 + d + 2000 $$.
While A freezes, B jumps 2000 seconds, so at time $$ t_0 + d + 2000 $$, system B will think this is actually $$ t_0 + d + 4000 $$.
